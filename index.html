Introduction

Eat more plants now, have lower cholesterol later.

Specifically, low density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, which is often described in the media and in doctor’s offices as “bad cholesterol.”

Elevelated levels of LDL cholesterol are associated with increased risk of cardiovascular disease and all cause mortality [Peng, 2022], resulting in many lost years of wellbeing and lifespan. Impacts of cardiovascular and other chronic diseases spread beyond individuals to their caregivers and to the broader community [Schwind, 2025]. It is in everyone’s best interest to promote reduction of chronic disease risk factors such as high LDL cholesterol.

Chronic disease is complex, and of the many factors that influence chronic disease risk, most (genetics, poverty, care access, luck) are not really conrollable. Disease management through medication is and has been an increasingly relied upon strategy in many places including the United Kingdom [Zhang, 2016], but that strategy is expensive, and access can be limited. Additionally, pharmaceutical manageament of chronic disease requires long-term medication consumption, which can have its own set of unserireable physiological side effects.

However, one chronic disease risk factor that individual people can take some control over is their food choices. Specific to this report, diet is known to impact LDL cholesterol levels and other chronic disease risk factors[Schoeneck, 2021].

This study examines the population of the United Kingdom (UK), drawing on diet diary data from a representative sample of UK households from 1974 to 2000, and disease risk factor rates (especially high LDL cholesterol) 1990 to 2023.

Ultimately, this study identifies a statistically significant inverse correlation between year-on-year changes in relatively-plant-based diet patterns and risk of high LDL cholesterol, at a five-year-time lag.

This is to say, eat more plants now, have lower LDL cholesterol five years later.

Background

Metabolic diseases are comprised of groups of disorders that disrupt the body’s ability to regulate energy and nutrient metabolism (NLM, 2025). Common conditions include obesity, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease. Together, these diseases correlate with a significant proportion of premature mortality worldwide. Looking at the UK, they are responsible for some of the highest healthcare spending in modern developed countries (le Roux, 2020).Not only can metabolic rate increase the risk of mortality, but it can also increase the cost of living in the form of prolonged healthcare. Therefore, it is essential to understand researched methods to lower such a rate.

Diet influences one’s metabolic rate through various physiological pathways. Diets high in saturated fats or refined carbohydrates tend to increase LDL cholesterol levels and promote chronic inflammation (Kumar et al., 2026). Conversely, diets richer in fruits, vegetables, and whole grains have been associated with reduced cardiovascular risk and improved lipid profiles. Understanding now the combinations of foods and nutrients to reduce metabolic risk is then even more important moving forward.

There have been numerous studies and controlled trials that dive into the relationship between dietary food groups and metabolic health. Rather than specific nutrient profiles, these studies have continually emphasized the overall patterns in diet that contributed to healthier rates of LDL cholesterol, namely a characterization of plant-derived foods (Barnard, 2017).

Although previous research has established strong connections between diet quality and decreased metabolic health. Fewer studies have examined how the long-term shifts of overall diet trends associate with metabolic rates at a nation-level scale. Exploration with the UK’s National Food Survey provides a unique opportunity to investigate these dietary changes and determine their relationship with metabolic disease, focusing on LDL cholesterol.

Data

Sources

Diet data

The primary explanatory data, the National Food Survey (NFS), was sourced from the UK Data Service. Access required institutional credentials. The data was pre-anonymized.

It spans 1974-2000, where it sampled ~7,000 households per year for a total of around 190,000 households. The survey looked into the “diet diary” during a 7-day period for each household. In the diary, the corresponding household would record their food purchasing information. Alongside the diary, the NFS collected the nutrient conversion information for each of the foods purchased. Meaning, there was a quantity value and nutrient unit respective to each food.

The demographics included in this report were, age, sex, and household composition. This was split into child/adult/eldery and male/female groups. These variables were used to create metrics allowing for per-person comparisons of the nutrients. These metrics weighted children as 0.5, adult women as 0.8 and elderly as 0.85 of a male adult in terms of their caloric consumption.

Disease data

The primary response variable data was acquired from the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) looking at the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study ranging from 1990 to 2023. The data was specifically selected from an open API to find the summary exposure value risk rates: LDL Cholesterol, Body Mass Index, Fasting Plasma Glucose Systolic Blood Pressure, Kidney Dysfunction, Low Bone Mineral Density, and Metabolic Risks. These rates were preaggregated with the API ingestion by year, government region in the UK and to population per region.

Pipeline

NFS data was downloaded as zip, with nested folders for every year from 1974 to 2000. Each yearly folder had space separeted .txt files, and all years shared reference tables. Year by year, diet diary data was joined to household data (to extract number of people in the household and location within the UK), and then joined with provided nutrient composition information for each food (minfd ->majfd explain ?). Food entries in the diary were from an initial ~180 options, which were sorted into 16 food groups. From there, data was aggregated on a household by household basis according to nutrient profile, and according to food group profile.

Aggregated data was exposed to the machine learning pipeline described in the Analysis section, resulting in nutrient profile and food group profile cluster assignments for each household throughout the timespan of the data.

Proportional distribution of these clusters was joined with the disease data on year and region. Disease data from the IHME was obtained in a preprocessed, preaggreagted .csv file.

Units

Food groups rates were normalized as a proportion of total daily calories consumed. Each food has an empirically established amount of calories per mass coefficient, and for each household, the quantity in appropriate mass units for each food entry was multiplied by that coefficent and then divided by the total calories for that household.

The nutrient groups were normalized as quantity per daily calories. There are a variety of appropriate units on a nutrient by nutrient basis, so each nutrient was calculated by that unit divided by calories.

Food group and nurtient normalized ratios were z-scored before analysis, with the mean being set to zero and a single standard deviation being set to one, resulting in a more coherent vector space for machine learning.

The disease rates from the preaggregated IHME data are summary exposure values (SEV) risk rates.

k-means clustering

In k-means clustering, data points are categorized into k number of clusters.

The best, most stable k for the aggregated household diet data was determined by a cross validation proces where subsamples of the data were iteratively clustered in comparable pairs, outputing an Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) score, which rated that pairing of clusters by assessing cluster label overlap (1.0 is perfect overlap, a decent number of clusters should have ARI score as close to 1.0 as possble). The k number of clusters with the highest ARI score was then selected as the “best” k number of clusters for k-means clustering. For food group aggregated data, the best k number of clusters was consistently found to be 2.

The principal components that were required to explain 95% variance in the aggregated foodgroup data were fed into an additional round of PCA to produce two derivative principal compenents, which were in turn fed into a t-distributed stochastic neighbor emedding (t-SNE) visualization. The t-SNE plot projects 20 dimensional nutrient space in 2 dimensions, enabling visualization of the k-means cluster assignments for each household.

Results

Based on the consisitent decline in its SEV risk rate, high LDL cholesterol was chosen as the chronic disease symptom of primary interest.

Year-on-year change in nutrient profile cluster 2 was assessed against a time-lag sweep of year-on-year change in SEV risk rate for LDL cholesterol using a cross correlation function (CCF), resulting in Pearson correlation score r. Perfect correlation is r =1.0, whereas no correlation is r =0.0.

A stasticially significant correlation (p <0.05) was noted between 2.5 and 7.5 years, and an even more significant correlation was found at precisely 5 years (p < 0.01). Peak correlation at 5 years was over 0.6.

The five year time lag restricted the number of years for continued analysis. Because the diet data only goes until 2000, and the disease data begins in 1990, the maximal appropriate overlap window was diet data from 1985 to 2000 correlated with disease data from 1990 to 2005.

Correlation validation

For those 15 years, the time points were shuffled through 10,000 random permutations, and reassessed with cross correlation. All random permutations failed to produce statistically significant correlation, suggesting that the detected correlation between nutrient cluster 2 and high LDL cholesterol at a five year time lag was a legitimate signal in the data.

Observed r = 0.679, n = 15, permutation p-value = 0.0065

Conclusions

Overall conclusion

There appears to be a statistically significant inverse correlation between nutrient profile cluster 2 and five-year time lag SEV risk rates for high LDL cholesterol. Nutrient profile cluster 2 is characterized by higher levels of nutrients most associated with plant-based foods, so we conclude that there is a detectable correlative association between plant-based food trends and lowering LDL cholesterol rates in the UK population.

Of course, this is consistent with years of literature and medical advice. The innovation of this project, if any, is the identification of a five year time lag specifically between plant-based diet trends and LDL cholestrol rates. This information is potentially useful to any individual tracking their own health, and to public health officials assessing the effectiveness of policiies related to food and metabolic disease.

Limitations

It might be expected that this five year time lag finding would apply to any population, not just the UK. That said, there are certainly many confounding variables involved in human health outcomes –genetics, poverty, food access, education, environmental exposures, other lifestyle factors, and more – so a strong proclaimation about the universal applicability of this finding is not warranted. Even looking only at the UK only, there are many caveats to consider in this data. The food diaries were self reported, so the information might be of varying reliability. Also, the diet diaries tracked the food that was purchased. It is assumed that the purchased food was consumed, but that could be inaccurate. Relatedly, some households might be bulk shoppers who buy a lot of food every other week. For such a bulk shopping household, the timing of the diet diary week could make them appear to consume way too much or way too little.

Final thoughts

Human health is complex, the association with diet, while well studied, is also complex. Any additional insight into connections between food and health can bolster individuals and society in general to improve their longevity and reduce their risk of chroic disease. While there are mixed messages from online influencers about these food health connections, this analysis suggests that the mainstream medical messaging is right: a more plant based diet can help lower LDL cholesterol.

So once again:

Eat more plants now, have lower cholesterol later. Specifically LDL cholesterol, and specifically five years later.
