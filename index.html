Introduction

Can changes in population-level dietary trends track with downstream changes in population-level health outcomes?

Specifically, can a population’s increase in a relatively plant-based dietary pattern be correlated with a time-lagged decrease in that population’s elevated LDL cholesterol risk level? If so, at how long a time lag?

This study examines the population of the United Kingdom (UK), drawing on diet diary data from a representative sample of UK households from 1974 to 2000, and disease risk factor rates (especially high LDL cholesterol) from 1990 to 2023. Correlation is derived from aggregates at national and regional levels.

Ultimately, this study identifies a statistically significant inverse correlation between population-level year-on-year changes in relatively-plant-based diet patterns and risk of high LDL cholesterol, at a five-year-time lag.

Background

LDL cholesterol is often described in the media and in doctor’s offices as “bad cholesterol.” Elevated levels of LDL cholesterol are associated with increased risk of cardiovascular disease, resulting in many lost years of wellbeing and lifespan [Peng, 2022].

This study focuses on population level LDL cholesterol risk rates specifically, but it is important to note that this is just one risk factor in a more complex public health picture around chronic metabolic disease.

Chronic metabolic diseases are comprised of groups of disorders that disrupt the body’s ability to regulate energy and nutrient metabolism [NLM, 2025]fix this citation?. Common conditions include obesity, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease. Together, these diseases correlate with a significant proportion of premature mortality worldwide [need citation here too?].

Looking at the UK at beyond, chronic metabolic disease is responsible for some of the highest healthcare spending in modern developed countries [le Roux, 2020]. Impacts of cardiovascular and other chronic diseases spread beyond individuals to their caregivers and to the broader community [Schwind, 2025].

Chronic disease is complex, and of the many factors that influence chronic disease risk, most (genetics, poverty, care access, luck) are not really conrollable.

Disease management through medication is and has been an increasingly relied upon strategy in many places including the United Kingdom [Zhang, 2016], but that strategy is expensive, and access can be limited. Additionally, pharmaceutical manageament of chronic disease requires long-term medication consumption, which can have its own set of unserireable physiological side effects.

However, one chronic disease risk factor that individual people can take some control over is their food choices. Specific to this report, diet is known to impact LDL cholesterol levels and other chronic disease risk factors[Schoeneck, 2021].

Dietary factors have been found to influence chronic disease risk factors through various physiological pathways: diets high in saturated fats or refined carbohydrates tend to increase LDL cholesterol levels and promote chronic inflammation [Kumar et al., 2026]; Conversely, diets richer in fruits, vegetables, and whole grains have been associated with reduced cardiovascular risk and improved lipid profiles [Barnard, 2017].

Although previous research has pointed at contemporaneous connections between diet quality and chronic metabolic health, fewer studies have examined time-lagged correlation of population-level diet trends with population-level chronic metabolic disease risk rates.

While the results presented herein are explicitly pertinent to aggregate population-level trends, it would not be unreasonable for an individual person to take some inspiration from these findings. A population is a collection of individuals, after all.

More importantly though, population-level statistical findings are certainly appropriate for public health officials. Better information about time-lagged population-level diet and disease correlations can be used to inform policy making and progress monitoring for diet oriented public health initiatives. “““““““““““”

Data

Sources

The primary explanatory data, the National Food Survey (NFS), was sourced from the UK Data Service. It spans 1974-2000, where it sampled ~7,000 households per year for a total of around 190,000 households. The survey looked into the “diet diary” during a 7-day period for each household. In the diary, the corresponding household would record their food purchasing information. Alongside the diary, the NFS collected the nutrient conversion information for each of the foods purchased. Meaning, there was a quantity value and nutrient unit respective to each food.

The demographics included in this report were, age, sex, and household composition. This was split into child/adult/eldery and male/female groups. These variables were used to create metrics allowing for per-person comparisons of the nutrients. These metrics weighted children as 0.5, adult women as 0.8 and elderly as 0.85 of a male adult in terms of their caloric consumption.

The primary response variable data was acquired from the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) looking at the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study ranging from(1990 to 2023). The data was specifically selected from an open API to find the summary exposure value risk rates: LDL Cholesterol, Body Mass Index, Fasting Plasma Glucose Systolic Blood Pressure, Kidney Dysfunction, Low Bone Mineral Density, and Metabolic Risks. These rates were preaggregated with the API ingestion by year, government region in the UK and to population per region.

Pipeline

The NFS datasets were aggregated by household per year where they were vertically joined to create a continuous span from 1974-2000. The disease risk rates were horizontally joined on government region and year.

Units

Food groups rates were normalized as a proportion of total calories consumed. The nutrient groups were normalized as quantity per daily calories. The disease rates were summary exposure values (SEV) which are relative to the total possible risk.

Ethics and Responsible Use

The data is used under a non-commercial educational license, and comes both anonymized and pre-aggregated by the service providers to remove personally identifiable information (PII) and ensure confidentiality.

Analysis - Food Groups

Aggregations by food group and aggregations by nutrient were assessed with principle component analysis (PCA) and k-means clustering.

The entire set of ~190,000 household were analyzed for PCA and k-means clustering, but t-SNE visual renderings use a smaller sample size to enable more rapid report iteration.

FOOD GROUPS

PCA (principal component analysis)

The most explanatory component (PC 1) accounted for about 11% of variance in the food group aggregations.

The other components only explained ~5% variance, leading to 14 components required to cumulatively explain 95% of variance.

The axis of greatest variance, PC 1, orients to a more plant-based pole, and a more meat and refined food based-pole. PC 2 appears to orient to a more processed and refined foods pole, and a whole foods pole.

k-means clustering

In k-means clustering, data points are categorized into k number of clusters.

The best, most stable k for the aggregated household diet data was determined by a cross validation proces where subsamples of the data were iteratively clustered in comparable pairs, outputing an Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) score, which rated that pairing of clusters by assessing cluster label overlap (1.0 is perfect overlap, a decent number of clusters should have ARI score as close to 1.0 as possble). The k number of clusters with the highest ARI score was then selected as the “best” k number of clusters for k-means clustering. For food group aggregated data, the best k number of clusters was consistently found to be 2.

The principal components that were required to explain 95% variance in the aggregated foodgroup data were fed into an additional round of PCA to produce two derivative principal compenents, which were in turn fed into a t-distributed stochastic neighbor emedding (t-SNE) visualization. The t-SNE plot projects 20 dimensional nutrient space in 2 dimensions, enabling visualization of the k-means cluster assignments for each household.

Analysis - Nutrients

Additionally, we looked at nutrient profiles.

NUTRIENTS

PCA for nutrient profiles

For household nutrient profiles, PC 1 and PC 2 each explain over 20% of variance, and 11 components are required to cumulatively explain 95% of variance.

PC 1 for household nutrient profiles has a fat (saturated and unsaturated) pole and a “plant-food” pole (vegetable protein and carbohydrates and vitamins). PC 2 for household nutrient profiles has a high carbohydrate pole juxtaposed against a high animal protein pole.

For household nutrient profiles, best k number of clusters was consistently found to be 5. Notably, one of the five clusters has only about 5,000 household throughout the dataset. This small cluster turns out to be households that consumed a lot of organ meat.

Assessed against PC 1, cluster 0 is closer to the high fat pole, while cluster 2 is closely associated with the plant-like pole.

Analysis - Clusters over Time

Composition Clusters with Bar Graphs and Line Plots

These visualizations detail the change in proportion of each nutrient cluster over time. They demonstrate a slight decrease for cluster 0 from around 25% to 15%. Cluster 1 was relatively stable at around 3%. Cluster 2 showed an increase in proportion from around 3% to 40% with a relatively steep slope. Cluster 3 hobbled around the 20% proportion area, starting a few points above and then ending at a value right around 15%. Lastly, we saw cluster 4 start as the highest proportion, nearly 50% and slowly reduce to 30%. This informs us, as time continued, the trends mapped to cluster 2 becoming more prevalent as 0, 3, and 4 fell. This initial model motivated statistical checks to be ran to ensure the changes in proportion over timer (1974-2000) were statistically significant and not simply attributed to random noise.

Stats for Proportions Confirming the Significance of Changing Clusters Over Time

A two proportion test was conducted to investigate the year by year trends for each cluster to determine their significance.

Cluster 0 shows multiple statistically significant decline scattered throughout the time span. There are no significant reversals of this trend, so this cluster demonstrates slow, step-wise decline.

Cluster 1 stayed stable. It had few significant shifts and the overall proportion of its diet ownership suggests it has no strong directional trend.

Cluster 2 starts tiny and continuously grows to end with a diet proportion of 40%. It is the most dynamic cluster with string of statistically significant upward changes. It can confidently be said there is a significant strong positive trend in in its dietary proportion represented in households.

Cluster 3 fluctuates around 25-28%, it has volatility in 1990s, but beyond these outliers, it remains generally stable with fewer significant year to year changes.

Cluster 4 has the largest initial share of diet. It consistently has downward trends, when disregarding the shock during 1990-1992. It has a sustained statistically significant downward trend over the time period.


==== Year-over-Year Cluster Proportion Tests ====

Cochran-Armitage Test

The Cochran-Armitage trend test was applied separately to each of the five clusters, testing whether that cluster’s share of the population trended consistently upward or downward across the ordered sequence of survey years (1974-2000). It binary checked if the change in households was significantly different between the initial and final years to mark long-term trends. It found that all but cluster 1 experience significant change during the time frame. The confirms the listed trends, focusing on cluster 2 are not defined by noise in the data.Together, these results indicate a substantial compositional shift in the population’s cluster membership over the 27-year period, with Cluster 2 gaining population share at the expense of Clusters 0, 3, and 4, while Cluster 1 remained a stable, low-prevalence group throughout.

Analysis - Disease

LDL cholesterol and chronic disease data

Rates of metabolic syndrom related chronic disease symptoms in the UK from 1990 to 2020

Looking at the UK as a whole, SEV risk rates for high LDL cholesterol and high systolic blood pressure both show a downtrend from 1990 to 2000, althgouh high systolic blood pressure rates do start to climb again after that. Metabolic risk overall continues to increase throughout the timespane, along with high body mass index (BMI).

There is some regional variation in SEV risk rates over time for these chronic disease symptoms, but the only notable anomaly is the rate of high BMI in Wales compared to other regions. Wales has about 50% high BMI SEV risk for the whole dataset, whereas other regions do not approach that level of risk until later years, if at all.

Conclusions

Overall conclusion

There appears to be a statistically significant inverse correlation between nutrient profile cluster 2 and five-year time lag SEV risk rates for high LDL cholesterol. Nutrient profile cluster 2 is characterized by higher levels of nutrients most associated with plant-based foods, so we conclude that there is a detectable correlative association between plant-based food trends and lowering LDL cholesterol rates in the UK population.

Of course, this is consistent with years of literature and medical advice. The innovation of this project, if any, is the identification of a five year time lag specifically between plant-based diet trends and LDL cholestrol rates. This information is potentially useful to any individual tracking their own health, and to public health officials assessing the effectiveness of policiies related to food and metabolic disease.

Limitations

It might be expected that this five year time lag finding would apply to any population, not just the UK. That said, there are certainly many confounding variables involved in human health outcomes –genetics, poverty, food access, education, environmental exposures, other lifestyle factors, and more – so a strong proclaimation about the universal applicability of this finding is not warranted. Even looking only at the UK only, there are many caveats to consider in this data. The food diaries were self reported, so the information might be of varying reliability. Also, the diet diaries tracked the food that was purchased. It is assumed that the purchased food was consumed, but that could be inaccurate. Relatedly, some households might be bulk shoppers who buy a lot of food every other week. For such a bulk shopping household, the timing of the diet diary week could make them appear to consume way too much or way too little.

Final thoughts

Human health is complex, the association with diet, while well studied, is also complex. Any additional insight into connections between food and health can bolster individuals and society in general to improve their longevity and reduce their risk of chroic disease. While there are mixed messages from online influencers about these food health connections, this analysis suggests that the mainstream medical messaging is right: a more plant based diet can help lower LDL cholesterol.

So once again:

Eat more plants now, have lower cholesterol later. Specifically LDL cholesterol, and specifically five years later.
