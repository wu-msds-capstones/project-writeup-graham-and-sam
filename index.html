Introduction

Can changes in population-level dietary trends track with downstream changes in population-level health outcomes?

Specifically, can a population’s increase in a relatively plant-based dietary pattern be correlated with a time-lagged decrease in that population’s elevated LDL cholesterol risk level? If so, at how long a time lag?

This study examines the population of the United Kingdom (UK), drawing on diet diary data from a representative sample of nearly 190,000 UK households from 1974 to 2000, and population-level chronic disease risk factor rates (especially high LDL cholesterol) from 1990 to 2023.

Diet diary data is assessed by both food group profile and nutrient profile. Each household’s reported food acquisition is aggregated and normalized against that household’s total calories, painting a quantity-agnostic picture of that household’s diet composition.

A machine-learning pipeline is then applied to the household-level data, with a cross-validated stability optimized k-means clustering algorithm designating a cluster identity for each household, and with principal component analysis being used to orient and characterize the clustering.

The year-on-year changes to the population-level proportion of households assigned to any of these k-means clusters over time can serve as the explanatory variable for cross-correlative analysis against downstream population-level disease risk rates.

Ultimately, this study identifies a statistically significant inverse correlation between population-level year-on-year changes in relatively plant-based dietary patterns and risk of high LDL cholesterol, at a five-year time lag.

Background

LDL cholesterol is often described in the media and in doctor’s offices as “bad cholesterol.” Elevated levels of LDL cholesterol are associated with increased risk of cardiovascular disease, resulting in many lost years of wellbeing and lifespan (Peng et al. 2022).

This study focuses specifically on population-level LDL cholesterol risk rates, but it is important to note that this is just one risk factor in a more complex public health picture around chronic metabolic disease.

Chronic metabolic diseases are comprised of groups of disorders that disrupt the body’s ability to regulate energy and nutrient metabolism (“Metabolic Disorders” n.d.). Common conditions include obesity, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease.

Looking at the UK and beyond, chronic metabolic disease is responsible for some of the highest healthcare spending in modern developed countries (Roux et al. 2021). Impacts of cardiovascular and other chronic diseases spread beyond individuals to their caregivers and to the broader community (Schwind et al. 2025).

Chronic disease is complex, and of the many factors that influence chronic disease risk, most (genetics, poverty, care access, luck) are not really controllable.

Disease management through medication is and has been an increasingly relied upon strategy in many places including the United Kingdom (Zhang et al. 2016), but that strategy is expensive, and access can be limited. Additionally, pharmaceutical management of chronic disease requires long-term medication consumption, which can have its own set of undesireable physiological side effects.

However, one chronic disease risk factor that individual people can take some control over is their food choices. Specific to this report, diet is known to impact LDL cholesterol levels and other chronic disease risk factors (Schoeneck and Iggman 2021).

Dietary factors have been found to influence chronic disease risk factors through various physiological pathways: diets high in saturated fats or refined carbohydrates tend to increase LDL cholesterol levels and promote chronic inflammation (Kumar et al. n.d.). Conversely, diets richer in fruits, vegetables, and whole grains have been associated with reduced cardiovascular risk and improved lipid profiles (Kahleova, Levin, and Barnard 2017).

Although previous research has pointed at contemporaneous connections between diet quality and chronic metabolic health, fewer studies have examined time-lagged correlation of population-level diet trends with population-level chronic metabolic disease risk rates.

While the results presented herein are explicitly pertinent to aggregate population-level trends, it would not be unreasonable for an individual person to take some inspiration from these findings. A population is a collection of individuals, after all.

More importantly though, population-level statistical findings are certainly appropriate for public health officials. Better information about time-lagged population-level diet and disease correlations can be used to inform policy making and progress monitoring for diet-oriented public health initiatives.

Data

Diet data:
National Food Survey
(1974 to 2000)

All dietary data for this project originates from the UK government’s National Food Survey (NFS).

The NFS started in 1940 and eventually evolved into the Living Costs and Food Survey, which is active in 2026. However, not all of the associated data is publicly available.

For interested parties with institutional credentials, the UK Data Service does provide a curated batch of NFS data that spans from 1974-2000.

In this data, approximately 7,000 households per year across the UK were chosen at random from voter registration records and asked to participate by keeping a one week diet diary. From 1974 to 2000, almost 190,000 total households participated.

Consenting households tracked all food purchases for one week, recording the consumable quantity (cq) of each purchase and assigning it to one of over 300 minor food codes (minfd). Each minor food code can be mapped to one of 184 major food codes (majfd).

There are several native food group mappings included with the NFS data, but those food groupings tend to focus on economic details more than nutrition oriented details.

An iterative manual review of all reported foods in the data was conducted, and a custom set of sixteen foodgroups was designated based on literature (Schulz, Oluwagbemigun, and Nöthlings 2021; Perraud et al. 2022; Gaesser 2020) in order to assess the data from a nutritional perspective.

Each row in the diet diaries was assigned one of these custom food group labels, enabling food group profiling of each household. A full printout of the custom food group mappings is included in the Appendix .

Additionally, the NFS included annually adjusted nutrient conversion information for each of the minor food codes, allowing the recorded type and quantity to be converted into a nutrient profile for that purchase, and in turn aggregated to an overall nutrient profile for each household.

Demographic and economic information is also included with the NFS data. However, this analysis assesses diet composition per calorie, not diet quantity per capita. While calorie-per-person-per-day was used to cull data-devoid households in the data cleaning (discussed below), the food group and nutrient profile metrics carried forward into the analysis are quantity-agnostic.

So, the only demographic information that was ultimately used in this analysis is the regional location of each household.

Disease data:
Global Burden of Disease
(1990 to 2023)

Population-level disease risk rates for High LDL cholesterol and other metabolic-disease associated risk factors were acquired from the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME), using their Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Results tool.

The specific metric chosen for disease risk rate was summary exposure value, or SEV.

The IHME defines SEV as a “… measure of a population’s exposure to a risk factor that takes into account the extent of exposure by risk level and the severity of that risk’s contribution to disease burden.” Upon examination of the available metrics from the GBD Results tool, SEV appeared to be the best to assess risk factor prevelance.

Chronic disease risk factors for which SEV information was available included high LDL cholesterol, high body mass index, high fasting plasma glucose, high systolic blood pressure, kidney Dysfunction, low bone mineral density, and overall metabolic risks. These risk rates were hand-selected for all available years and obtained preaggregated at a population level, grouped by UK region and by year. For the scope of this project, all ages and both sexes were included in SEV information.

Dietary Data Engineering

Household food group profiles

For each diet diary row, calories were calculated based on NFS provided nutrient conversion factor for Energy (kCal) for the given food multiplied by the consumable quantity cq reported (Equation 1).

\[ calories_{row} = cq_{row} \times \text{energy conversion factor}_{row} \tag{1}\]

Then, for each household, calories were aggregated for each of the assigned food groups, and then divided by total calories for that household (Equation 2).

\[ {calorie\_ratio}_{food group} = \frac{\text{total calories per food group}}{\text{total calories per household}} \tag{2}\]

This resulted in a ‘calorie ratio’ for each food group for that household for that week. This normalized metric cancels out quantity, and purely examines the qualitative composition of each household’s reported food acquisition for the week. Therefore, the number of people in the household do not factor into this metric, and further demographic considerations did not need to be calculated.

Finally, calorie ratios for all food groups were normalized by Standard Scaler and z-scored, with the means set to 0 and the standard deviations set to plus or minus 1.

Household nutrient profiles

Total calories were calculated for each household as with the food group feature engineering.

Then, each minor food code minfd was converted to the nutrient-profile for that food multiplied by the quantity reported cq. This effectively expanded each diary entry row into twenty rows, one for each of the twenty nutrients tracked throughout the data, with the appropriate unit quantity for each nutrient reported (Equation 3).

\[ nutrient\_quantity_{row} = cq_{row} \times \text{nutrient conversion factor}_{row} \tag{3}\]

Maintaining each nutrient’s native unit (grams, milligrams, micrograms, etc.), the total quantity of each nutrient across all food purchases was aggregated for each household. Those quantities were then divided by total calories for that household (Equation 4).

\[ nutrient\_quantity\_per\_total\_cal_{row} = \frac{\text{total quantity per nutrient}_{row}}{\text{total calories per household}_{row}} \tag{4}\] The resultant nutrient-profile metric, ‘quantity per total calories,’ is similar in spirit to the ‘calorie-ratio’ metric from the food groups.

However, a pure calorie comparison for nutrients would not be viable, as certain nutrients (carbohydrates, fats) have abundant calories while other nutrients (vitamins) have essentially zero calories. Hence, native nutrient units were maintained and divided by the common total calories. This might preclude comparison amongst nutrients, but this analysis assesses household nutrient profiles, so the variation in fundamental units was not problematic.

Furthermore, variations in magnitude amongst the derived nutrient-quantity-per-total-calorie units were standardized as a z-score, with the mean for each dimension set to 0 and a single standard deviation set to plus or minus 1.

Data Pipeline Overview

As described in detail below:

  • Diet diary data was cleaned and aggregated by food group and by nutrient profile for each household in the dataset.
  • This yielded a sixteen-dimensional food group vector and a twenty-dimensional nutrient vector for each household.
  • Those vectors were subjected to a machine learning pipeline that assigned a food group cluster label and a nutrient cluster label to each household. Principal component analysis was used to understand and visualize the clustering.
  • Changing proportions of those clusters in the total population were assessed over time using a variety of statistical tests, in addition to visual inspection of time-based line plots.
  • Population-level SEV risk rates were then joined at varying time-lags on year and region.
  • The year-on-year rate of change for a given cluster proportion was correlated with year-on-year rate of change for metabolic disease risk factors at varying time lags.

Diet data cleaning

The NFS evolved over time, and this evolution prompted several data cleaning decisions for this analysis.

Alcohol, confectioneries, and soft drinks were added as reportable food options in 1992. It is presumed that some UK households did purchase alcohol, confectioneries, and soft drinks before 1992, but the diet diaries cannot show this. All three categories are considered high-calorie, so their sudden appearance does skew calorie-adjusted calculations. In order to maintain consistency over time, all diet diary rows from these three categories were dropped from analysis, though the related households and all their other diet diary entries were retained.

Initial analysis before dropping alcohol had designated alcohol as its own food group, for 17 total food groups. After dropping all alcohol rows, this analysis looked at 16 total foodgroups.

Northern Ireland was only included in the survey starting in 1996. In the interest of consistency, Northern Ireland households were excluded from this analysis altogether.

The nutrient conversion information evolved over time as well, tracking 21 nutrients in 1974 and over 40 nutrients by 2000. As with pruning rows from late-added foods and locations, nutrient trends were only assessed for the nutrients that were tracked throughout the entire dataset.

One of the included nutrients was Energy in MJ (megaJoules), which was collinear with Energy in kCal. Energy in MJ was dropped from nutrient analysis, resulting in 20 assessed nutrients.

The NFS and IHME data have a discrepancy in UK government office regions as well: the 1974 to 2000 NFS data list Merseyside as a distinct region, while the 1990 to 2023 IHME data includes that same geographical area as part of North West England. So, before aggregation, all NFS households from Merseyside were reassigned as being part of North West England, for consistency between datasets.

A final cleaning step was to prune households that reported a physiologically unrealistic amount of calories, below a threshold that people require to live. These households were considered to have provided unreliable data and were excluded from analysis. The caloric floor threshold was set to two standard deviations below the mean of log-space per capita calorie intake, inspired by similar approaches in the literature (Black 2000). While the overall analysis does not rely on per capita calculations, this pruning step does. Per capita per household calculations were adjusted for age and sex (adult males = 1, adult females = 0.8, children = 0.5, elderly adults = 0.85), consistent with the literature (“SACN Dietary Reference Values for Energy” n.d.).

Diet data aggregation

Dietary data aggregation was undertaken to generate the features described with Equation 1, Equation 2, Equation 3, and Equation 4 above.

The raw download from the UK Data Service has a folder for each year between 1974 and 2000, with space separated text files for diet diary, household information, and nutrient conversion factors. All years of the NFS data share minor to major food code look up tables with consistent food code descriptions.

Household food group profile aggregation

On a year by year basis, calorie-specific nutrient conversion information was joined to each row of the diet diary (on minor food code, minfd), multiplied by consumable quantity cq, and then summed per household to calculate total calories for that household (Equation 1). The calculated total for household calories was later joined back to un-aggregated data as needed.

Minor food codes were mapped to major food codes, which were then mapped to custom food group labels (see Appendix). Then each row was grouped by household and by foodgroup, aggregating total calories for each food group and for each household. From there, every household could be assigned a proportion of total calories that was attributable to each foodgroup Equation 2.

Household information was then joined on household number (hhno) to obtain the correct government office region for each household.

Finally, each year’s processed aggregated food group profile data was appended to a list of dataframes and vertically concatenated.

Household nutrient profile aggregation

Year by year, nutrient conversion information for all included nutrients was joined to each row of the diet diary (on minor food code, minfd), multiplied by consumable quantity cq, and then summed by household to calculate quantity of that nutrient for that household (Equation 3). This automatically included total calories for that household, which was then used to create quantity-per-total-calorie metrics for each household and each nutrient Equation 4.

Household information was then joined on household number hhno to obtain the correct government office region gor for each household.

Finally, each year’s processed aggregated nutrient profile data was appended to a list of dataframes and vertically concatenated.

Machine Learning Pipeline

The data for both food group and nutrient profiles were pivoted to a wide format, with each household retaining a row, and each column becoming one of the foodgroups or nutrients. The values in the wide pivot table were the household’s calorie ratio or quantity-per-total-calorie for that food group or nutrient, respectively. The wide tables were then z-score normalized.

The machine learning pipeline (described in detail in the Analysis section) assigned food group profile cluster labels and nutrient profile cluster labels to each household.

Proportions of each cluster type amongst all households were then plotted and assessed over time, nationally and regionally. Changes in these proportions over time are the fundamental explanatory variable of this analysis.

Joining disease data

Once food group and nutrient profile cluster labels were assigned and population-level proportions of those clusters were tracked over time, the IHME data with SEV rates for several metabolic risk factors was joined on region and year.

Since this analysis assesses time-lag, the IHME data was joined across a sweep of lag years, so that for a given diet data year, columns for corresponding SEV rates for a range of lag years (zero years to up to 49 years) were added.

Project scalability

The exact aggregation scripts that transform the raw diet diary data into usable machine learning inputs are specific to the conventions of the 1974 to 2000 NFS data collection process. Ingestion of other similar dietary data would not automatically work with those scripts.

That said, modifications to the ingestion and aggregation scripts could facilitate ingestion of applicable similar data. Once ingested and aggregated according to the feature engineering steps described above, harmonizable diet data from other sources (the UK in later years, the United States, or other countries) could be integrated into the analysis. If appropriately prepared, it could potentially be clustered by the ML pipeline in this project and analyzed downstream as if it were from the original data.

The chronic disease data from the GBD Results tool is easily scalable, and compatible chronic disease SEV data for other developed nations could be obtained and integrated into this analysis.

All that said, due diligence would be required to make sure any new dietary data was actually harmonizable, and potential external confounding factors would have to be sought and ruled out.

Data Ethics

Responsible Use

The diet data is used under a non-commercial educational license, and comes both anonymized and pre-aggregated at a household level by the service providers to remove personally identifiable information (PII) and ensure confidentiality. The disease data, sourced from the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) is anonymized at a government region level and is void of any PII. Both datasets are without risks of re-identification.

The protection comes from specific choices from the service providers and not a general asurance. The household identifier (hhno) for the NFS data is pseudonymized prior to its posting, and the geographic granularity is limited to the respective Government Office Region and not a postal code to further reduce this risk. The IHME data is a collection of estimates that are published at a sub-national level and contain no patient level records. In the analysis, no actions were taken to merge these datasets with another with any intention of re-identification. There are no tables or cells in this analysis that can be of risk of identification for any specific household. All use of the datasets in this report are upholding the ters of use for both the IHME and UK Data Service.

Bias

The National Food Survey primarily tracks household food purchases intended for home preparation, which historically could undercount the food consumed outside the household. Additionally, during the 27-year period, food-category definitions and household participation patterns may have changed. This means some portions of the observed cluster trends could reflect changes in the methodology as opposed to genuine diet transition.

As well, some household types could be over or under surveyed and the resulting data could have patterns more specified to those unique areas as opposed to a holistic view of the UK. The survey data itself was self-reported, and therefore is open to social desirability bias. Food groups with a negative connotation may be underrepresented and conversely, foods with a positive connotation may be overrepresented.

The clustering methodology introduced an additional source of bias worth addressing. The clusters, the principal component, the normalization per calorie and the esclusion of alcohol and confections were all modeling choices that shaped the found patterns. It should be understood that the specified clusters were produced by the listed choices and are not clinically validated diet categories.

Ethical Implications

Diet data can be easily connected to judgements about socioeconomic status, and other hereditary health situations. The findings from this study are presented as described trends as opposed to evaluative claims. For this reason, no causal claims can be made, only correlative suggestions can be presented.

The distinction is worth noting because food choices are often shaped substantially by structural factors such as cost, availability or policy. The choices are often not enforced by preference alone. As such, no cluster in this report should be regarded as “better” or “worse” for the set of choices made by the respective households or the regions they belong to.

Additionally, no causal claim can be made linking the early 1990s recession (discussed below) to the change in the respective nutrient cluster groups.

The findings in this report work to suggest dietary trends that may introduce benefits to health at a population-wide scale.

The findings are not for commercial application or targeted marketing. Since the data utilized in this study followed guidelines and restrictions, these guidelines should continue with any further use of the analysis produced.

Methods

Software and Tools

All data engineering, analysis, and visualization were conducted in Python 3.13.7 and R 4.5.1.

Python packages that were used in the final analysis include pandas and numpy for basic data manipulation, scikit-learn for PCA and k-means clustering, and matplotlib, seaborn and geopandas for visualization. R packages that were used in the final analysis include tidyverse for data manipulation, rstatix/coin for statistical testing, and ggplot2 for visualization.

Principal component analysis

Z-scored household diet data was subjected to dimensionality reduction in both the food group dimension space and the nutrient dimension space using principal component analysis, or PCA. PCA finds the multi-dimensional axis of greatest variance for all the data in a dataset, and multiple iterations of PCA can cumulatively explain increasing amounts of variance in the data.

For both food group and nutrients dimension spaces, PCA was successively run until 95% of the variance was explained.

The top two most explanatory principal components (PC 1 and PC 2) for each analysis were plotted in order of their linear combination weights for the given dimensions, producing a human interpretable orientation for that component.

Best k selector for k-means clustering

In k-means clustering, data points are categorized into k number of clusters, based on data point distance from moving cluster centroids across many initializations of the algorithm. Any target number of clusters could be arbitrarily selected, but rigorous analysis requires a more careful process to select the most stable number of clusters for the data.

In order to select the most stable number of clusters in a rigorous manner, a cross-validation cluster stability overlap function was constructed and named best-k-selector. In this algorithm, a random sample of the data points is selected and subjected to k-means clustering in successive rounds with the number of clusters increasing iteratively upwards from two. Each round involves 80% of the sample being subsampled, clustered, replaced, and then a fresh 80% subsample (with many of the same data points) being clustered at the same number of clusters. The clustered subsampled data points are compared for overlapping labels. The percentage of datapoints with overlapping cluster labels between the two subsamples is called the Adjusted Rand Index, or ARI score.

The best-k-selector repeats the process as it sweeps from two clusters upward as many times as instructed. Eventually, the ARI scores for each number of clusters is averaged, and the number of clusters with the highest average ARI amongst all repeats is considered the ‘best k’ number of clusters for that dataset.

k-means clustering

Once the ‘best k’ number of clusters was consistently discovered for food group and nutrient dimensions, clustering of all households in the data could take place.

The k-means clustering algorithm places the desired number of cluster centroids randomly in the data, assigns each data point a cluster label based on the nearest centroid, shifts the centroids to the average position of their respective assigned data points. This is repeated over and over until the centroids effectively stop moving. The k-means algorithm reinitializes numerous times (hundreds or more) to assess cluster label consistency before determining the final designated cluster labels for each data point.

PCA to visualize clusters

In order to visualize household cluster labels from 16-dimensional food group space or 20-dimensional nutrient space, the principal components required to cumulatively explain 95% of variance of the data were put through an additional round of PCA, this time aiming for exactly two new principal components. These new components were used as the X and Y axes along which to plot all households in a 2-dimensional scatter plot projection.

This double PCA approach does not directly inform the k-means clustering, but it does allow the datapoints to be visualized in human-friendly two dimensions, and along reasonably human-interpretable axes.

PCA used for heat map interpretation

Heatmaps are used to visualize the z-scores of cluster centroids.

The most explanatory principal components (PC 1 and PC 2) from upstream analysis are used to order the axes of heatmaps for each cluster.

Cluster proportions over time

Analysis of changes in the proportions of clusters over time was conducted using two proportion z-tests, Cochran-Armitage tests, and Kendall Tau Trend tests. Additionally, the cluster proportions were visualized as lineplots.

The two proportion z-test was applied to each pair of consecutive years survey years for every cluster. This provided a localized check of significant changes from one year to next next. This test was chosen because it made no assumption about the change of the shape overtime. It was able to catch short-term shifts that a braoder trend test may miss or average out. It was also leveraged to identify and validate statistical changes over the course of the time span which could then be addressed later.

Because the two proportion z-tests are not meant to characterize a trend across the 27 year period, the Cochran-Armitage test was applied to cover this shortfall. It was applied to each cluster’s proportion change over the full time frame. The test was specifically selected because cluster membership was effectively binary at each point in time and the survey years followed a chronological order so the conditions were optimal for this test. Once more, it was used to determine if a cluster’s proportion moved consistently in one direction across the study as opposed to looking at noise driven changes.

The Kendall’s tau rank correlation was introduced as a distribution-free companion check for the Cochran-Armitage test which relies on a count-based and approximately linear structure. When these two tests agree on the direction and significance of a trend, that agreement acts as strengthened confidence that such a pattern is genuine.

Cross correlation function (CCF) for time lag

After k-means clustering, each UK region’s proportion of each assigned cluster per year was exported into a parquet file, which was loaded into a new pandas dataframe. That dataframe was extended with many lag year columns onto which IHME disease SEV data could be joined on region and year, with given diet data lag years corresponding to an IHME disease SEV actual years.

This enabled rate of change correlation assessment, with year-on-year changes in cluster proportions and in SEV for a given metabolic risk being assigned a Pearson correlation coefficient r, with high correlation being closer to 1, and no correlation being closer to zero.

The main explanatory variable (changes in population-level proportion of a particularly plant-oriented nutrient cluster over time) and the main response variable (changes in SEV for High LDL cholesterol) were specifically selected based on their visual appearance in lineplots (Figure 10 and ?@fig-metabolic-rates-over-time), and not based on more rigorous mathematical means.

This is noted in the Methods section because an important part of this lag correlation analysis is that it was primarily informed by a visibly remarkable pair of trends in the lineplots.

Correlation validation

After identifying statistically significant correlation between the explanatory and response variables at a particular time lag, the order of SEV rates by year was held constant, and tested for correlation against thousands of permutations of the pertinent diet cluster data years. If the identified correlation was actually a product of random chance, it would be expected that other permutations of the data would randomly produce similar appearance of correlation some of the time.

Additionally and finally, correlation rates at the identified time lag were plotted in a choropleth of the UK, showing how consistently nutrient cluster proportion changes and metabolic risk factor SEVs are correlated at the identified time lag over the time span of the data.

Analysis

By food groups

PCA

The most explanatory component (PC 1) accounted for about 11% of variance in the food group aggregations as seen in Figure 1.

The other components only explained about 5% variance each, leading to 14 components required to cumulatively explain 95% of variance.

As seen in Figure 2, the axis of greatest variance for all households by food-group calorie ratios orients to a more plant-based pole (higher in fruits and vegetables and whole grains), and a more meat and refined-food-based pole (higher in saturated fat and refined grains). PC 2 for all households by food group calorie ratios also appears to orient to a more processed and refined foods pole, and to a whole foods pole.

k-means clustering

A 2-dimensional projection of the household clustering from 16-dimensional food group calorie ratio space is presented in Figure 3. The principal components that were required to explain 95% variance in the aggregated foodgroup data were fed into an additional round of PCA to produce two derivative principal components. These two components serve as the x and y axes in Figure 3. There are twice as many households assigned to Cluster 0. The data is continuous, so the clusters appear adjacent, though the boundary between the two is rather distinct.

The heatmap in Figure 4 combines information from PCA and k-means clustering. The two columns represent the two clusters that were assigned in k-means clustering. The axis of the heatmap is oriented along PC 1 for food group aggregations. Cell color and printed number inside the cell indicate cluster centroid z-score for each dimension. Blue cells are negative z-scores, white cells are neutral, cluster-colored cells are higher z-scores.

The axis of greatest variance only explained 11% of variance in the food group model, so this visualization is presented with some caution. That said, it is notable that the axis of greater variance appears to orient along a ‘healthy food’ and an ‘unhealthy food’ pole.

By nutrients

PCA

As seen in Figure 5, PC 1 and PC 2 for household nutrient-quantity-per-total-calories profiles each explain over 20% of variance, and 11 components are required to cumulatively explain 95% of variance.

As seen in Figure 6, PC 1 for household nutrient profiles has a fat (saturated and unsaturated) pole and a “plant-food” pole (vegetable protein and carbohydrates and vitamins). PC 2 for household nutrient profiles has a high carbohydrate pole juxtaposed against a high animal protein pole. These nutrient profile principal components are somewhat more compelling than the food group principal components, as they each explain over 20% of household variance.

k-means clustering

For household nutrient profiles, best k number of clusters was consistently found to be 5. Notably, one of the five clusters has only about 5,000 households amongst the almost 190,000 households in the dataset. This small cluster turns out to be households that consumed a lot of organ meat.

The clusters depicted in Figure 7 show most of the UK households falling into Clusters 0, 2, 3, and 4, with a slim minority being assigned to Cluster 1. This study chose to focus on Cluster 2 going forward, but exploration of the outlier Cluster 1 did reveal that these were households that reported purchasing organ meats (such as kidney and liver).

The heatmaps in Figure 8 and Figure 9 allow characterization of the five nutrient profile clusters. Cluster 0 appears to be defined by the high fat pole of PC 1. Cluster 2 is defined by negative values in the high fat pole of PC 1, and by positive values in the plant-nutrient pole of PC 1. Cluster 3 appears to be oriented to the high animal nutrient pole of PC 2, while being lower in carbohydrates. Cluster 4 is characterized by high carbohydrates, while being relatively low in other nutrients.

As noted above, Cluster 1, characterized by high retinol, was found to be households that purchased organ meats.

Nutrient cluster proportions over time

The most striking finding from k-means clustering (paired with PCA for interpretability) was that nutrient-profile Cluster 2, the more plant-oriented cluster, increased its proportional share of the UK population dramatically from 1974 to 2000. Insofar as this analysis was seeking a strong diet-cluster signal to serve as the explanatory variable, nutrient-profile Cluster 2 seemed to be the best candidate.

Therefore, the rest of this analysis focuses on nutrient-profile clustering, and not food-group clustering.

Figure 10 details the change in proportion of each nutrient cluster over time.

There is a slight decrease for Cluster 0 from around 25% to 15%. Cluster 1 was relatively stable at around 3%. Cluster 2 showed an increase in proportion from around 3% to 40% with a relatively steep slope. Cluster 3 remained around the 20% proportion area, starting a few points above and then ending at a value right around 15%. Lastly, cluster 4 starts as the highest proportion, nearly 50%, and slowly reduces to 30%.

This suggests that as time continued, the trends mapped to cluster 2 became more prevalent as proportions of clusters 0, 3, and 4 fell.

This initial model motivated statistical checks to ensure the changes in proportion over time (1974-2000) were statistically significant and not simply attributed to random noise.

Statistics

Cluster change summary

A series of two-proportion z-tests assessed the significance of the year-over-year nutrient group cluster composition changes over the 27-year time period, as displayed in Table 1.

Cluster 0 demonstrated a gradual contraction over the time period. It had 8 statistically significant annual shifts (30.8% of transitions). There was a scattered, but downward aiming shift without stark reversals upward.

Cluster 1 exhibits consistent stability with the lowest volatility among the clusters. It only had 6 statistically significant transitions (23.1% of the shifts). There was not a specific trajectory instead, there were both positive and negative changes during the time span.

Cluster 2 was the most dynamic cluster, noting a robust upward trend. Beginning with a baseline composition of around 3%, there was a leap to 40% of household diet share by the end of the period. A majority of year-over-year changes reach statistical significance (15 transitions or 57.7%) confirming a strong continued expansion.

Cluster 3 fluctuates around 25-28% of household share. It was heavily influenced in the early 1990s (plausibly due to a recession in the UK during this time period) with some volatility. Beyond this disruption, the trend remained broadly stable with 8 statistically significant transitions (30.8%).

Cluster 4 began with the largest share of household diet share, but experienced consistent downward pressure. Disregarding the market shock in the early 1990s, the group had 11 statistically significant year-over-year transitions (42.3%) backing this claim.

Cochran-Armitage Test

The Cochran-Armitage trend test, displayed in Table 2, was selected because it is designed to detect whether a proportion moved consistently in one direction across an ordered sequence of groups. The cluster membership was essentially binary (a household either belonged to a given cluster or did not), and survey years followed a natural chronological order, making this data well suited for this test.

The test was applied separately to each of the five clusters, to see whether that cluster’s share of the population trended consistently either upward or downward across the survey years (1974-2000). The test incorporated every year to determine whether the year-to-year movement followed a single consistent direction, as opposed to random fluctuation. It found that all but Cluster 1 experienced significant change during the time frame, confirming that the trends observed in these clusters reflected a real, sustained shift rather than noise in the data. These results demonstrated a substantial compositional shift in the UK population’s cluster membership over the 27-year period, with Cluster 2 gaining population share at the expense of Clusters 0, 3, and 4, while Cluster 1 remained a stable, low-prevalence group throughout.

Companion Robustness: Kendall Tau Trend Test

The Kendall’s Tau trend test, depicted in Table 3, was employed as a companion check to the Cochran-Armitage test. The Cochran-Armitage test models trends through a counts structure that was approximately linear. The Kendall test does not operate with the same assumptions, thus it is a warranted robustness check for clusters with a nonconforming structure. The Kendall test ranked the years and determined if the direction of the change was consistent. It was applied to each cluster’s composition proportion. It looked at more than just year-to-year changes and worked to give a more comprehensive view of the trends. It found the same pattern as the Cochran-Armitage test: every cluster except for Cluster 1 showed consistent monotonic movement with the corresponding sign to the previous test.

The agreement of the tests supports the conclusion that there are compositional shifts in the diet clusters, namely that Cluster 2 increased for this UK population during the years of analysis.

LDL cholesterol and chronic disease data

Chronic metabolic disease SEV rates for the UK at large were plotted over time.

As seen in Figure 11, high LDL cholesterol decreased between 1990 and 2023. Based on this visualization, year-on-year change in population-level high LDL cholesterol SEV was selected as the response variable for analysis. Population-level SEV for high systolic blood pressure also went down from 1990 to 2010, but then started increasing again afterward, suggesting confounding factors and ruling it out for further exploration in this study.

Notably, despite the decrease in population SEV for high LDL cholesterol, overall metabolic risk in the UK has steadily climbed over this timespan.

Results

While exploratory analysis ranged beyond the final figures included above in this report, time-lag correlation studies were focused on population-level changes in the proportion of nutrient-profile Cluster 2 Figure 10, and on population-level LDL cholesterol SEV scores ?@fig-metabolic-rates-over-time. The results reported below are in reference to the correlative association between those two selected variables.

Diet and Disease time-lag correlation

With population-level year-on-year changes in nutrient cluster 2 selected to be the explanatory variable, and with population-level year-on-year changes in high LDL cholesterol SEV selected to be the response variable, a correlation analysis was conducted from 0 to 20 years of time lag using a cross correlation function (CCF). For each lag year pair, a Pearson correlation coefficient r was generated.

A perfect correlation would have a Pearson correlation coefficient r of 1.0, whereas a non-correlation would be expected to have a coefficient r closer to 0.0. The CCF swept through 20 years of lag options, resulting in n = 21 year-lag year pairs for the CCF. Based on this number of assessed year-lag year pairs, the 95% significance threshold was calculated to be approximately r = 0.43, and the 99% significant threshold was calculated to be approximately r = 0.562.

\[ r_{crit,\,95\%} = \frac{1.96}{\sqrt{n}} \tag{5}\]

\[ r_{crit,\,99\%} = \frac{5.67}{\sqrt{n}} \tag{6}\]

Cross correlation function

As seen in Figure 12, there was a sustained correlation above the 95% significance threshold between 2.5 and 7.5 years, and a distinct peak above the 99% significance threshold at 5 years.

Proceeding with a 5 year time-lag, there are 15 possible years of overlap between the diet and disease data sets: NFS diet data from 1985 to 2000 corresponds at a five year time lag with GBD disease data from 1990 to 2005.

Correlation validation

This finding was tested against thousands of random permutations to investigate if the apparent correlation might just be coincidence. The order of SEV scores for the 15 years of time-lagged GBD disease data was held constant, and the corresponding 15 years of nutrient cluster 2 proportions were randomly shuffled. Correlation was assessed with each permutation, and the observed r from Figure 12 of 0.679 was found to be a more extreme result than over 99% of correlations.

Observed r = 0.679, n = 15, permutation p-value = 0.0065

Conclusions

Overall conclusion

There appears to be a statistically-significant inverse correlation between year-on-year changes in population proportion of nutrient-profile Cluster 2 and year-on-year changes in population-level high LDL cholesterol SEV scores, at a five-year time lag. Nutrient profile cluster 2 is characterized by higher levels of nutrients most associated with plant-based foods, so we conclude that there appears to be a detectable time-lagged correlative association between increasing plant-based food trends and lowering LDL cholesterol rates in the UK population.

Of course, this is consistent with years of literature and medical advice. The innovation of this project, if any, is the identification of the five-year time lag specifically. This information is potentially useful to any individual tracking their own health, and to public health officials assessing the effectiveness of policies related to food and metabolic disease.

Limitations

The most significant limitation of this study is that it is not longitudinal. It would be ideal to track high numbers of individual people in a scientifically controlled manner, over many years of analysis. In that case, varying dietary habits and health outcomes could be mapped to individual people and then aggregated for statistical power. Unfortunately, longitudinal data like this is rare and hard to obtain. So, the best option for this realm of investigation was to find aggregatable data with large numbers of subjects, and to make statistically cautious population level claims from there. Future investigation in this direction would be buoyed by access to longitudinal diet and health data.

It might be expected that this five year time lag finding would apply to any population, not just the UK. That said, there are certainly many confounding variables involved in human health outcomes —– genetics, poverty, food access, education, environmental exposures, other lifestyle factors, and more — so a strong proclamation about the universal applicability of this finding is not warranted.

Even looking at the UK only, there are many caveats to consider in this data. The food diaries were self-reported, so the information might be of varying reliability.

Also, the diet diaries tracked quantities of consumable food that was purchased. It is assumed that all of this purchased food was actually consumed, but that could be inaccurate.

Relatedly, some households might be bulk shoppers who buy a lot of food every other week. For such a bulk shopping household, the timing of the diet diary week could make them appear to consume way too much or way too little.

Final thoughts

Human health is complex, the association with diet, while well studied, is also complex. Any additional insight into connections between food and health can bolster individuals and society in general to improve their longevity and reduce their risk of chronic disease. While there are mixed messages from online influencers about these food health connections, this analysis suggests that the mainstream medical messaging is right: a more plant-based diet might help lower LDL cholesterol.

To be very clear, this study makes no claims about diet and health for individual people. That said, this study does conclude that at least in the UK from 1985 to 2000, there was a statistically significant correlation between the year-on-year rate of change of the population proportion of nutrient-profile Cluster 2 (as designated by k-means clustering) and the year-on-year rate of change for high LDL cholesterol SEV score.
